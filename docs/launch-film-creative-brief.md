# Open CRM launch film

## Status and decision

This is the public-safe creative brief for the launch film. It is written for
the agents and people who will storyboard, render, edit, review, and publish
the film. It is not a product specification and it does not authorise claims
that the product does not currently support.

The release target is:

> Small, early-stage B2B teams (roughly 2–20 people) already working with
> coding agents and several live projects, who want customer memory they can
> run locally, inspect, change, and hand to the agent harness they already use.

The first viewer is a founder, product lead, or technical operator who has
notes in too many places, has tried a CRM that became administration, and is
already comfortable opening a terminal. The second viewer is the agent that
will be asked to operate the workspace. The film must make sense to both.

The public name remains **Open CRM**. Use this descriptor on the end card:

> An open-source agentic relationship manager.

“ARM” can be introduced as the category. Do not lead the film with OSARM or
claim that Open CRM is the first product in a market. The longer name is useful
as a naming exploration, but it is not yet the clearest public promise.

## The promise

> Your customer memory becomes a working folder your agents can read, reason
> about, and improve—with you approving every change.

The film should answer five questions without requiring a product tour:

1. Where does the information live? In a folder the team owns.
2. What does the agent do? It reads source notes and prepares grounded work.
3. What does the person do? They inspect the evidence and approve or reject.
4. What happens after approval? The next action becomes part of the account
   record, with its source and owner visible.
5. Why use it across projects? The same loop works for sales, customer care,
   relationships, and product feedback without becoming a black box.

The emotional shift is simple: “I have to maintain another system” becomes
“I can give my agent the memory and review the work it prepares.”

## The story in one paragraph

A founder finishes a call and drops the note into a local workspace. Instead of
opening a heavy CRM and retyping the meeting, they ask the coding agent to read
the note, identify what is actually known, and prepare the one next action that
the evidence supports. The agent shows its sources, its uncertainty, its owner,
and its proposed task. The founder approves it. The account now carries a
traceable next move. A second and third account show that the same pattern
works for an onboarding problem and a relationship change. A recurring signal
can then be handed to product work, without pretending the CRM made the product
decision. The last frame makes the ownership model explicit: open code, local
records, the team’s choice of agent, and a human at the write boundary.

## What the viewer must understand in ten seconds

The opening must establish a problem and a point of view before showing a
feature:

> The context is already there. Your CRM should work like the rest of your
> codebase.

Then show the loop in one clean sentence:

> Source note → agent proposal → human approval → accountable next action.

If a viewer watches only the first ten seconds, they should know this is not a
hosted CRM advertisement, not an autonomous sales bot, and not a dashboard of
unexplained scores. It is a local, source-grounded workspace for a person and
their agent.

## Hero film: 72-second narrative

The hero cut is the primary launch asset. It should be designed at 16:9,
1920×1080, with caption-safe compositions. A 40-second cut already exists in
`tmp/open-crm-launch-film/v2`; retain its factual workflow and use this brief
to give the longer film a stronger beginning, clearer examples, and a more
useful ending.

### The 40-second cut

The current short cut should remain useful as a README and social asset:

- 0–4: Less CRM upkeep. More follow-through.
- 4–12: Ask an external agent to read a note and prepare changes.
- 12–22: Inspect the evidence, approve one proposal, see the record change.
- 22–28: Open, local, yours; show the harness choices.
- 28–34: Feedback becomes a reviewable product-work candidate.
- 34–40: Open CRM and the repository CTA.

The short cut should not try to carry all three examples. It should carry one
complete source-to-approval loop, then earn the right to name the broader
system.

### Cutdowns

The 15-second cut should have one job: create recognition and curiosity.

1. “The context is already there.”
2. A note becomes a source-grounded proposal.
3. A person approves it.
4. “Open CRM — your local, agent-ready relationship manager.”
5. “Start with one account.”

Make a 9:16 version for short-form feeds and a 1:1 version for repository and
social previews. Captions are mandatory. A silent version must tell the whole
story through motion, labels, and state changes.

## The example pack

All records in the film are synthetic. Use names that are obviously fictitious
or safe `.example` domains. Never use a private Zentrik account, customer,
person, transcript, URL, workspace id, or internal phrase.

### 1. Relay Labs — commercial follow-through

Source note: “The buyer wants SSO and a security review before the pilot. Maya
will coordinate the review, but no date is confirmed.”

Agent proposal:

- task: confirm the security-review owner and propose two dates;
- owner: Maya;
- evidence: the dated call note;
- missing: confirmed date and the buyer’s security contact.

Human result: approve the task. Do not depict an email being sent or a deal
advancing automatically.

Why it is first: it is immediately familiar, has a clear next action, and
demonstrates the difference between what was said and what is still unknown.

### 2. Northstar Robotics — customer care

Source note: “Two new users stopped at the same onboarding step while importing
their catalogue.”

Agent proposal:

- task: reproduce the import step and record the exact failure;
- owner: Sam;
- evidence: the support note and its attachment reference;
- missing: browser version and a reproducible sample.

Human result: approve an investigation task. Do not turn support evidence into
a product roadmap commitment inside the CRM.

Why it is second: it proves that Open CRM is not only for selling.

### 3. Orbit Finance — relationship change

Source note: “The champion moved to another team. The original project has no
confirmed owner after the reorganisation.”

Agent proposal:

- task: verify the current project owner and update the contact record;
- owner: Priya;
- evidence: the dated relationship note;
- missing: the new decision-maker.

Human result: approve the research task, not a guessed stage change.

Why it is third: it shows people and account memory working together.

### 4. Kiteworks Studio — product feedback

Source note: “CSV export needs field mapping for the team’s existing report.”

Agent proposal:

- candidate: recurring export-mapping problem;
- sources: the two synthetic account notes that mention it;
- next step: prepare a source bundle for product review;
- boundary: no roadmap decision and no feature release.

Why it is last: it demonstrates the improvement loop without pretending that
Open CRM is a product-planning system.

## Agent interaction choreography

The agent must look useful because it is constrained, not because it is
omniscient. Use a three-column composition:

1. **Source** — the note, date, and source kind.
2. **Reasoned proposal** — concise evidence, proposed action, missing
   information, and confidence. Never display private chain-of-thought.
3. **Review** — the exact operation, owner, date or trigger, and Approve /
   Reject.

The agent prompt should be short enough to read in a film:

> Read the latest source note. Propose one next action, cite the source, and
> say what is missing. Do not contact anyone.

The response should be equally short:

> I found one supported action. The owner is known; the date is not. Ready for
> review.

Show the command or MCP boundary as a small label, not as a technical lecture:

> Same folder · checked operation · review mode

The interaction is illustrative. The film may render an external chat panel,
but it must not imply that Open CRM contains an embedded chat, that it has a
private provider integration, or that an agent can bypass Review.

## Visual language

The film and product should share one visual system. Use the real local fonts
and semantic tokens from `src/tokens.css` and `src/fonts.css` rather than
inventing a second brand language:

- Fraunces for the few large editorial statements;
- Inter for interface labels and readable prose;
- IBM Plex Mono for ids, commands, dates, and counts;
- teal for selection and primary action;
- violet for agent work;
- green for a confirmed result;
- amber for waiting, unknown, or needs-review state;
- quiet paper surfaces, restrained borders, and one 24px content gutter.

Motion rules:

- Every transition must travel from source → context → proposal → decision →
  record. Never animate a number before its cause is visible.
- Use a steady camera push, drawer movement, or card reflow. Avoid particles,
  glowing neural nets, spinning dashboards, and frantic cursor movement.
- Let the human approval be the strongest visual beat. It is the product’s
  trust proposition.
- Keep important copy on screen long enough to read with sound muted. Do not
  use text as decoration that disappears on the beat.
- Use a single coordinate system for all scenes so the product feels like one
  place, even when the camera moves between the agent view and the app.

Sound rules:

- The existing second cut uses a restrained original instrumental score at 120
  BPM. Keep it as the base unless a better score is commissioned.
- Structure the score in 4- and 8-bar phrases. Put clear accents at the hook,
  agent request, approval, and end card.
- Add quiet UI sounds: a key press, a source drawer opening, and one approval
  chime. Sound should reward a decision, not imply autonomous activity.
- Ship a music version, a fully silent captioned version, and a version with
  captions burned in for feeds that do not preserve sidecar captions.

## Production architecture

Use deterministic, inspectable production for all product proof:

- Storybook or a small browser-rendered scene harness for account, source,
  proposal, review, and Book states;
- Playwright for fixed viewport screenshots and frame capture;
- FFmpeg for frame sequencing, audio muxing, captions, H.264 delivery, and
  quality checks;
- a single frame clock, fixed font loading, and synthetic fixtures checked into
  the film source package;
- an optional generative or “deep” visual tool only for an abstract opener or
  texture, never for product UI, data, people, or a claim that needs to be
  trusted;
- MP4/H.264 for distribution and a high-quality mezzanine such as ProRes when
  the editor needs to continue working. “MPG” is not a required delivery
  format.

The render must be reproducible from a clean checkout. Do not make the product
build depend on the film. Keep film sources and temporary renders under an
ignored `tmp/` directory or a separate public asset package, and keep the
tracked brief and manifest free of private data.

Each render should emit:

- 1920×1080, 24 or 30 fps hero master;
- 1080×1920 vertical cutdown;
- 1080×1080 square cutdown;
- silent and music versions;
- six to eight keyframes/contact sheet;
- `film-manifest.json` with scene ids, synthetic-data declaration, dimensions,
  frame rate, audio provenance, destination files, and claims checklist;
- a short README with the exact render and verification commands.

## Public-truth guardrails

The film may say:

- local-first and folder-owned, when showing the documented folder workflow;
- agents such as Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, and other shell/MCP harnesses can
  work on the same records;
- every fact can show its source;
- agents propose and people approve;
- product feedback can be captured locally and handed on as evidence.

The film must not say or imply:

- autonomous feature shipping, autonomous outreach, or automatic customer
  messaging;
- an embedded in-app agent chat when the panel is only an illustrative
  external-harness view;
- live calendar, mail, call-recorder, or cloud feedback synchronisation unless
  the exact public build supports it;
- that the product knows a buyer’s intent, revenue, stage, or next date when the
  source does not say so;
- “self-evolving” as an already-live autonomous system. Use “reviewable
  improvement loop” instead;
- “first”, “only”, or market-leadership claims;
- real customer or internal Zentrik data.

The creative lead should run a final claim audit with the sound off. If a claim
is only true because narration explains a caveat, rewrite the visual.

## Distribution and use

Use the hero film on the repository landing path and launch page. Use the
40-second cut in the README only if it remains legible at its displayed size.
Use the 15-second cut for social posts and release notes. The hosted demo and
the first-use guide remain the conversion path; the film should point to them,
not replace them.

Recommended end-card layout:

```text
OPEN CRM
An open-source agentic relationship manager.

Start with one account.
Ask your agent to prepare the next move.

github.com/Zentrik-AI/zentrik-open-crm
Try the browser demo →
```

Do not put release-internal debate, private roadmap language, unverified
integration plans, or agent instructions in the film, title, description, or
commit history. The public description should stay as clear as the product:
open, local, source-grounded, and human-approved.

## Acceptance checklist

Before publishing, the film owner should be able to answer yes to all of these:

- [ ] A new viewer can explain the source → proposal → approval → action loop
      after one silent watch.
- [ ] The release target is recognisable as a small agent-native team, not an
      enterprise procurement department.
- [ ] One example is a complete account workflow and three examples explain why
      the product applies across existing projects.
- [ ] The agent is shown as an external harness and never as an unannounced
      in-app capability.
- [ ] The approval is individual, visible, and stronger than the animation
      around it.
- [ ] Every visible claim has a source in the public repository or is clearly
      labelled as illustrative.
- [ ] Every record, person, domain, note, and URL is synthetic or public-safe.
- [ ] The film does not promise autonomous sending, autonomous shipping, or
      live integrations the build does not provide.
- [ ] Captions, silent playback, reduced-motion-friendly playback, and mobile
      crops have been checked.
- [ ] Typography, colors, focus semantics, and spacing use the product tokens.
- [ ] The render can be reproduced from a clean checkout with FFmpeg and the
      documented browser capture command.
- [ ] `ffprobe` confirms the expected dimensions, frame rate, duration, and
      audio streams for every published variant.
- [ ] The final title, description, repository link, and commit contain no
      internal reasoning or private data.

## Tests worth running before choosing the final cut

Run small, deliberate tests rather than producing many expensive variants:

1. **Hook test:** scattered-context opening versus “your CRM should work like
   your codebase.” Measure which makes the audience describe the problem
   correctly, not which gets the most empty impressions.
2. **Proof test:** source-first layout versus agent-first layout. Keep the one
   that makes viewers understand why the proposal is trustworthy.
3. **CTA test:** “Start with one account” versus “Bring your agent.” The former
   should reduce the perceived setup cost; the latter should increase harness
   curiosity.
4. **Mute test:** show both cuts to someone who has not seen the product and
   ask them to narrate what changed. If they say “the AI updated the CRM”
   without mentioning approval, the cut fails.

The winning film is not the one with the most effects. It is the one that lets
an agent-native team recognise its current mess, see a safe first step, and
believe that Open CRM can become the memory layer for all of its projects.
